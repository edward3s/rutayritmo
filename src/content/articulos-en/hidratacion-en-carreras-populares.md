In running, people repeat "drink before you're thirsty." The advice is prudent on a hot day, but it can be bad advice if it turns into an obligation to drink liters. In long races, excess water without electrolytes can dilute the body's sodium and cause hyponatremia, a serious problem that doesn't announce itself.

The right hydration isn't a fixed amount: it's the amount that replaces what you lose without going over.

> **In short:** weigh yourself before and after an hour of running to estimate your sweat rate. Sip according to thirst and heat. In efforts longer than 90 minutes, combine water with a source of sodium and carbohydrates.

## Measure your sweat rate

Weigh yourself before and after a one-hour run, without drinking during the route and with clothing as similar as possible. The difference in weight, in grams, is roughly the sweat lost. If you lose a kilogram, you've lost about a liter. Add whatever you drank to refine the calculation.

One runner may lose 0.5 liters per hour, or 1.5 liters per hour under the same conditions. Copying a friend's bottle doesn't work. Repeat the test in summer and in winter: the rate changes with temperature and humidity.

## What to drink

For runs under 60-75 minutes, water is usually enough. For longer efforts or in high heat, a drink with electrolytes and carbohydrates can help replace sodium and energy. You don't need a sports drink for a 40-minute jog.

Salt isn't magic: if you sweat a lot and drink only water, you can end up with cramps and feeling unwell. If you take gels, wash them down with water to dilute them. And if you've never used electrolytes, try them in training.

## At aid stations

- Don't stop dead: slow down and drink while walking a few steps.
- Take small sips; a whole glass at once can upset your stomach.
- If there are bottles, you can carry one in your hand to the next point.
- Don't throw packaging outside the bins: besides littering, it can cause falls.
- If it's hot, pouring water on your head and neck helps you feel cooler, but it doesn't hydrate you.

## The risk of drinking too much

Hyponatremia occurs when blood sodium drops too low, often from drinking a lot of water without replacing sodium during long efforts. Symptoms such as nausea, headache, confusion, swelling, or disorientation require immediate medical attention. It isn't common, but it is serious.

The practical recommendation is not to drink more than you sweat and not to force liters because of an abstract rule. If you urinate frequently and very clear during a race, you may be drinking too much.

## Heat and altitude

In heat, your heart rate rises and your pace drops. Adjust the effort, not just your hydration. At altitude, thirst and fluid loss change too. If you're going to run in new conditions, reduce the intensity and pay attention to symptoms.

## After the race

Weigh yourself and drink until you've regained the weight you lost, spread over the following hours. If you've lost more than 2% of your body weight, prioritize rehydration before your next session. And don't use beer as a recovery drink: alcohol doesn't help.
