There are two schools: the one that replaces shoes every 500 kilometers and the one that wears them until they fall apart. Neither is absolutely right. Longevity depends on weight, surface, footstrike, foam, and above all on how your body feels. The odometer number helps, but it doesn't decide on its own.

> **In short:** there is no universal mileage. Look at the midsole, the outsole, and any discomfort. If the shoe has lost its cushioning and you start feeling impacts or pain, replace it.

## The signals that actually matter

**The midsole is compressed.** Bend the shoe at the midfoot and see whether the foam springs back. If deep creases remain and the material looks flattened, the cushioning is no longer doing its job.

**The outsole is worn unevenly.** Mild wear is normal. A hole at the heel or forefoot, or wear that exposes the midsole, means it's time to change.

**New aches appear.** If you start noticing impacts in your heel, pain in the sole of your foot, or a knee that complains on long runs, check your footwear before blaming your training. It isn't always the shoe, but it's an easy variable to rule out.

**The upper is torn at flex points.** A loose stitch on the side may be cosmetic; a tear that leaves your foot unsupported is not.

## The 500-kilometer myth

The 500-800 km figure is commercial guidance, not a law of physics. A shoe with modern foam may last longer on a lighter runner who trains on a track; another may lose its responsiveness sooner on a heavier runner training on asphalt. Temperature, sweat, and storage also play a role.

What does work is noting the purchase date and approximate kilometers. You don't need a spreadsheet: a note on your phone or a mark on the box. When you reach 600-700 km, start watching for the signals. If they're still fine at 900, don't throw them out over a number.

## Rotating two pairs

Alternating two pairs extends the life of both and lets the foam recover its shape between runs. It also keeps you from depending on a single shoe for everything: a more cushioned pair for long runs and a lighter one for intervals or races.

You don't need ten pairs. Two is a good starting point; three, if you train a lot and have the space. And don't keep your shoes in the trunk of your car: heat and humidity punish the materials.

## What to do with the old ones

If the shoe no longer cushions but the upper is intact, it can work for walking, for the yard, or for running errands. Don't use it for running if your body is telling you not to. Before throwing it away, check whether your local store or a nearby nonprofit collects athletic shoes for reuse.

## The decision in one sentence

If you have to ask yourself whether the shoe is dead, it probably already is. Comfort is the best test: if every run starts with a discomfort that wasn't there before, change your footwear and try it for two weeks. If the discomfort disappears, the answer was easy.
