Plantar fasciitis has a very recognizable symptom: the first steps after getting out of bed hurt in the sole of the foot, near the heel. After a few minutes, the discomfort fades and you can walk. That improvement is misleading, because it invites you to carry on as if nothing were wrong. And carrying on as if nothing were wrong is, often, what keeps it around.

> **In short:** it isn't just inflammation, and it isn't cured by an insole alone. Sensible treatment combines load adjustment, foot and calf strength, appropriate footwear, and patience. If the pain is intense or lasts for months, see a professional.

## How to recognize it

- Sharp pain on the inside of the heel, especially with your first steps in the morning.
- Discomfort that improves as you walk and returns after you've been sitting.
- Pain when you put weight on it after a long run or after standing for a long time.
- Stiffness in the sole of the foot and in the calf.
- Sometimes, pain in the arch or the outside of the foot.

Not all cases of plantar fasciitis hurt the same way, and not all start with the same symptom. The differential diagnosis includes other causes of heel pain, such as nerve compression or a stress fracture. That's why an assessment is worthwhile if it doesn't improve.

## Why it appears

The plantar fascia is a band of tissue that runs from the heel to the toes and helps support the arch. The problem usually appears when load increases faster than the tissue can adapt: more kilometers, more speed, more hard surfaces, a change of shoes, or a summer of sandals.

Ankle mobility, calf and foot strength, weight, and the time you spend on your feet also play a role. It isn't simply "having flat feet" or "walking wrong." It's a matter of capacity and load.

## What usually helps

**Adjust the load.** You don't have to stop moving, but you do need to reduce what irritates it: less volume, fewer hills, less speed, and more rest between sessions. Switch to cycling or swimming if running hurts.

**Work the calf and the foot.** Heel raises on both legs, then on one leg; toe curls with a towel; arch raises without curling the toes. Strength in the whole system improves the fascia's tolerance.

**Stretch the fascia and the calf gently.** A gentle stretch of the sole of the foot and the calf, without pain, can ease morning stiffness. Don't roll aggressively over the painful spot: it can irritate it further.

**Check your footwear.** A worn-out shoe, one that's very soft, or one with a very low drop can increase the load. A generic insole can provide temporary relief; if the problem persists, consider a custom one with a podiatrist.

**Use a night splint if your professional recommends it.** Keeping the fascia in a stretched position overnight can reduce morning pain in some cases. It isn't a universal solution.

## What usually doesn't work

- Resting for weeks doing nothing and then returning at exactly the same load.
- Rolling the sole with a ball until it hurts.
- Changing shoes every month looking for the magic fix.
- Repeated injections without a load and strength plan.
- Ignoring the pain because "it's only when I get up."

## Signs to see a professional

Ask for an assessment if the pain prevents you from walking normally, doesn't improve after four to six weeks of load adjustment, appears after a blow, or comes with numbness, tingling, or loss of strength. Also if you have diabetes or circulation problems.

Plantar fasciitis usually improves, but it takes time. Most cases respond to months of consistent work, not a week of rest. Haste is part of the problem; patience is part of the solution.
