Repeats are a tool, not a ritual. A 400 and a 1,000 can look like the same session with a different number in your notebook, but they ask for different things: one sharpens speed and economy; the other sustains aerobic effort for longer. Choosing wrong won't injure you on its own, but it leaves you tired without improving what you wanted.

> **In short:** 400s for speed and economy, with short recovery. 1,000s for aerobic power and 5K-10K pace, with longer recovery. Neither replaces the easy run.

## 400 meters: controlled speed

The 400 is run fast, but it is not a sprint. The pace is around 3K-5K pace, depending on the runner and the goal. Recovery is usually 60-90 seconds of jogging or walking, and total volume should not exceed 4-6 kilometers of work for a recreational runner.

It is used to:

- Improve running economy and stride.
- Touch speed without entering sprint territory.
- Prepare for a 5K or 10K.
- Break the monotony of a plan based only on easy runs.

Example for someone who wants to break 25 minutes in the 5K: 10 × 400 at 5K pace, with 75 seconds of jogging. If the last repetition falls off a lot, reduce to 8.

## 1,000 meters: sustaining the effort

The 1,000 is run at a more conservative pace than the 400, but more time accumulates per repetition. Recovery is usually 2-3 minutes and the number of repeats is 4 to 6. The stimulus is close to maximum oxygen uptake and 5K-10K race pace.

It is used to:

- Hold a high pace without stopping.
- Improve aerobic capacity.
- Practice race pace.
- Build confidence for a 10K or a half marathon.

Example: 5 × 1,000 at 10K pace, with 2 minutes of jogging. If your goal is a [half marathon](/en/diario/primera-media-maraton/), this type of session is closer to what you need than a 400.

## The quick comparison

| | 400 | 1,000 |
|---|---|---|
| Pace | 3K-5K | 5K-10K |
| Recovery | 60-90 s | 2-3 min |
| Typical repetitions | 8-12 | 4-6 |
| Quality volume | 3-5 km | 4-6 km |
| Main goal | Speed and economy | Aerobic power and pace |
| How it feels at the end | Fast, still breathing | Very taxed, legs gone |

## Which one to choose depending on the moment

If you're just starting, you don't need either one. First build a base with [easy runs and walk-run](/en/diario/empezar-a-correr-en-8-semanas/). Repeats come when you can already run 30-40 minutes without stopping and have done a few weeks of easy runs.

If you're preparing for a 5K, the 400 and the 1,000 complement each other. If you're preparing for a 10K or a half marathon, the 1,000 gains weight. If you're preparing for a marathon, longer repeats—1,000s, 2,000s or 10-15 minute blocks—make more sense than a downpour of 400s.

And if you only have one quality day a week, choose the session that most resembles your goal. You don't need to do it all.

## Recovery and surface

Recovery is part of the session. If you shorten the rest periods to "make it harder," you change the stimulus and arrive worse for the next repetition. Respect the plan and don't turn the recovery jog into a race.

Surface matters too. The track is fast and controlled; asphalt is accessible; dirt is kinder to the joints. Avoid doing repeats on steep hills if you haven't prepared for them before. And don't do the repeat session the day after a long run.

## A minimal quality plan

If you run three times a week, a simple structure works:

- An easy 40-50 minute run.
- A repeat or threshold session.
- A longer, easier run.

The rest is consistency. Repeats don't fix a week without sleep or a shoe with 1,200 kilometers on it. Before changing the plan, check whether you're sleeping and eating enough; almost always the problem isn't the session you're missing but the recovery you're short on.
