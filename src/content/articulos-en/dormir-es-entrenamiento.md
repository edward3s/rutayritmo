Training plans have a column for kilometers, another for pace, and sometimes one for strength. Almost none has a column for sleep. Yet sleep is when the body repairs muscle, consolidates motor learning, and regulates appetite and stress hormones. Training a lot and sleeping little is doing half the work.

> **In short:** try to sleep 7-9 hours on a regular schedule. If one night goes badly, don't panic: what matters is the weekly trend. A 20-minute nap can help; a two-hour nap can throw things off.

## What happens when you don't sleep

One bad night doesn't ruin a plan. A week of four or five hours can show: heart rate rises during efforts that used to feel easy, perceived effort increases, strength drops, and hunger gets out of sync. Coordination also worsens, which on trails or uneven asphalt is no small detail.

You don't need a ring to measure sleep to know you're accumulating a debt. If getting up is hard, you sleep less than seven hours on weekdays, and you reach the weekend exhausted, you already have the signal.

## A regular schedule

The body works better with a stable rhythm. Going to bed and getting up at the same time — even on weekends, within an hour — helps more than sleeping ten hours on Sunday and five on Monday. Morning light also helps reset the internal clock: open the blinds or go for a walk before looking at your phone.

## The pre-bed routine

- Lower the light intensity an hour before bed.
- Leave your phone out of bed or in airplane mode.
- Avoid caffeine after 2:00 p.m. if you're sensitive; it has a half-life of several hours.
- Eat a light dinner if you train late, but don't go to bed hungry.
- Alcohol helps you fall asleep, but it fragments deep sleep. It is not a recovery tool.
- If you train at night, leave at least an hour between the end of the session and bed.

## Naps

A 20-30 minute nap can restore alertness without affecting nighttime sleep. If you sleep more than 45 minutes, you may wake up with sleep inertia and feel worse in the afternoon. For those who train twice a day, a short nap after the first session is a useful tool, not a luxury.

## Sleep is not negotiable with caffeine

A coffee before a long run can help performance, but it doesn't replace lost hours. If you need three coffees to function, the problem isn't caffeine: it's rest. And if sleep is persistently disturbed, consult a professional; there may be medical causes.

## The week before the race

In the week of your [half marathon](/en/diario/primera-media-maraton/) or [marathon](/en/diario/primera-maraton-plan/), try to sleep a little more and keep a regular schedule. Don't leave rest until the night before: it's hard to sleep well when you're nervous. The accumulated debt from the week weighs more than one regular night.

The best training plan in the world doesn't make up for five hours of sleep. If you have to choose between one more run and one more hour in bed, bed almost always wins. Running is a sport of consistency, and consistency is built by resting.
