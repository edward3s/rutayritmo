Zone 2 has become the favorite topic of watches and of accounts that promise to improve your VO2max with walks. Beneath the noise is a simple and fairly solid idea: most of your training should be easy, and easy means you can talk while you run.

It isn't a secret zone that only switches on with a heart rate monitor. It's a low aerobic intensity, the one you use to build volume without creating fatigue that later keeps you from training well.

> **In short:** if you can hold a full conversation, you're in zone 2. Most of your kilometers should look like that. One or two hard sessions a week are enough.

## What it feels like

At first, zone 2 feels too slow. It's almost embarrassing. That's normal: the body is used to associating "training" with effort. Breathing should be nasal or nearly nasal, and you should be able to speak in full sentences without stopping to catch your breath.

If you're alone, try counting to ten out loud while you run. If you get to the end without gasping, you're doing fine. If you have to stop, slow down or walk for a minute.

## Why it works

Easy training improves the heart's ability to pump blood, mitochondrial density, and the efficiency with which you use fat as fuel. It also lets you accumulate more running time at a lower cost. A runner who does every easy run at a moderate pace isn't training more: they're training with more fatigue and worse quality in their hard sessions.

The 80/20 logic is easy to remember: about 80% of weekly time at easy intensity and 20% at moderate or high intensity. In practice, with three runs, two are easy and one includes quality work. You don't need to measure it to the minute.

## How to measure it without obsessing

- **Talk test:** full sentences, no gasping.
- **Heart rate:** useful as a reference, but it can shift 10-15 beats because of heat, coffee, or stress. Don't chase an exact number.
- **Pace:** it changes with the terrain. Uphill, zone 2 may be a minute per kilometer slower. That's fine.
- **Feel:** you should finish feeling like you could keep going. If you end up wrecked, it wasn't easy.

## The most common mistakes

**Running every easy run in zone 3.** It's the classic: neither easy nor hard, uncomfortable enough to tire you out and slow enough not to improve your speed. If in doubt, go slower.

**Running your long run all out.** The long run is an endurance session, not a race. If you finish it unable to talk, you've turned easy training into a competition against yourself.

**Ignoring the terrain.** A run with 300 meters of elevation gain isn't comparable to a flat one. Adjust the effort, not the ego.

**Not sleeping.** Zone 2 doesn't make up for six hours of sleep. If you're tired, your heart rate rises and the easy session becomes an ordeal. Read about [sleep as part of training](/en/diario/dormir-es-entrenamiento/).

## A sample week

For someone who runs 4 days a week and is training for a 10K:

| Day | Session | Intensity |
|---|---|---|
| Tuesday | 45 easy min | Zone 2 |
| Thursday | 3 × 8 min threshold | Moderate-high |
| Saturday | 30 easy min + strength | Zone 2 |
| Sunday | 70 easy min | Zone 2 |

80% of the volume is in the three easy sessions. The threshold session is the one that moves the needle. If one day you can't handle threshold, change the order: make it easy and save the quality for when you're whole.

## Zone 2 is not a religion

Some runners obsess over not exceeding a number and end up walking hills they could run. Zone 2 is a guide, not a law. On a long run with wind or heat, pace drops and heart rate rises; what matters is the effort, not the decimal.

And if you only have 30 minutes three times a week, you don't need a sophisticated plan. Run easy two days and make the third session a little livelier. Consistency beats precision.
