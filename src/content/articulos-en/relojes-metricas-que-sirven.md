A running watch can be a great tool or a machine for generating anxiety. The difference is not in the price, but in the metrics you look at. If every morning you decide how to train based on a recovery score you don't know the origin of, the watch has stopped being a coach and become a boss.

> **In short:** use the watch to measure time, distance, pace and, with caution, heart rate. Treat VO2max estimates, calories and recovery scores as guidance, not diagnoses.

## The metrics that do matter

**Time and distance.** They are the basis of any plan. GPS can lose signal between buildings or under trees; on routes with many turns, the distance comes up short. Don't obsess over the decimal.

**Average and per-kilometer pace.** Useful for comparing similar sessions and for not starting too fast. On hills or in wind, pace says little; look at effort.

**Heart rate.** It helps you learn how your body responds and keeps an easy run from turning into a race. The wrist sensor lags and fails in cold weather or when the strap is loose. If it matters to you, use a chest strap.

**Cadence.** There is no magic cadence of 180 steps per minute. It is a reference that can help shorten your stride and reduce bounce, but it is not a universal target. If your cadence is low and you have discomfort, talk to a professional.

## The ones to view with suspicion

**Estimated VO2max.** The watch calculates it from heart rate and pace, not from a lab test. It can shift because of heat, fatigue or a downhill run. Use it as a long-term trend, not as a grade for the day.

**Recovery score.** Each brand uses a different algorithm and doesn't always explain what it measures. If the score says "rest" but you feel fine, don't cancel training because of a number. And the opposite: if your body asks for rest, don't cover it up with a green metric.

**Calories.** They are an estimate. Don't use them to decide how much to eat after a long run.

**HRV from a single reading.** Heart rate variability can be useful with a long series of data and context, but a single reading doesn't tell you whether you're sick or simply slept badly.

## How to use data without being used by it

Look at weekly trends, not daily decisions. A week with a higher heart rate than usual can be heat, stress or lack of sleep; it is not a signal to change the entire plan. Note how you felt in each session: that information is worth more than any score.

Use the watch to execute the plan, not to improvise it. If the plan says 45 easy minutes, the watch shouldn't turn the run into a race to lower your average pace. And if one day you go out without a watch and enjoy it, that also counts as training.

## Battery, GPS and privacy

An accurate GPS drains battery. For long runs, use battery saver mode or a watch with enough battery life. Check your privacy settings: some apps publish your routes by default. If you run alone, hide the start and end of your routes.

## What you don't need

You don't need a 700-euro watch to start. A phone with an app and some headphones can be enough for months. If you buy one, prioritize comfort, battery and GPS accuracy; advanced metrics are an extra, not a necessity.

Before looking at the watch, look at how you're sleeping and how you feel. The best sensor is still your body, even if it doesn't have a screen.
