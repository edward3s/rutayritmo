The knee is the joint that gets the most complaints in a running log. It is also one of the hardest hit by self-diagnosis: shoes, asphalt, or footstrike get the blame when the problem usually lies in a change of load, strength, or recovery.

Not all knee pain is a serious injury. But there are signs you should not train through.

> **In short:** if your knee locks, swells, gives way, or the pain is intense and constant, stop and seek advice. If it is a mild discomfort that appears with load and improves with rest, review volume, strength, and footwear before continuing.

## Warning signs

Seek medical attention if any of these appear:

- Your knee locks or you can't straighten it fully.
- It swells quickly after a blow or a twist.
- The joint "gives way" or feels like it won't hold you.
- Intense pain at rest or at night.
- Fever, redness, or heat in the area.
- Pain after a trauma, even if it seemed mild at first.

These signs don't necessarily mean there is a serious injury, but they do mean a professional should assess them. It is not the time to try an easy run "to see what happens."

## Common causes

Without a diagnosis, the most common labels are:

- **Patellofemoral pain:** discomfort around the kneecap, often when walking downstairs or after sitting for a long time.
- **Iliotibial band syndrome:** pain on the outside of the knee, typical in runners who increase volume or run downhill.
- **Patellar tendinopathy:** pain in the tendon, below the kneecap, that gets worse with jumping and hills.
- **Meniscus or ligament injury:** usually associated with a specific twist or impact and with mechanical symptoms.

Don't try to decide which one you have with an internet search. Location and history help, but a physical examination is what guides the diagnosis.

## What to do in the meantime

**Reduce the load, don't eliminate it.** If running hurts, switch to walking, cycling, or swimming for a few days. Prolonged complete rest is usually not the best strategy for a tendon or patellofemoral pain, but forcing it isn't either.

**Avoid what triggers the pain.** Downhills, stairs, deep squats, or pace changes can irritate the area. It isn't forever; it's while the discomfort settles.

**Work on strength.** Quadriceps, glutes, and calves are the foundation. Leg raises, glute bridges, box squats, and heel raises are a good starting point. Start with a small range of motion and no pain.

**Check your footwear.** A very worn shoe or a recent change in drop may coincide with the discomfort. It isn't the only cause, but it's easy to check. The guide to [shoes with 900 km](/en/diario/zapatillas-900-kilometros/) explains the signs.

**Sleep and eat.** Recovery is not a luxury. An increase in volume during a week with little sleep is a common combination for discomfort.

## Returning to running

A practical rule: if the pain is mild and doesn't change your running form, you can try a short, easy run. If your knee is worse the next day, you progressed too fast. Go back to walking or cycling and try again in 48 hours.

Start with 15-20 minutes on flat ground at an easy pace, and add 5 minutes every two or three runs. If the pain always appears at the same kilometer, don't ignore it: it may be a sign that the load exceeds your current capacity.

## When to seek help

If the pain lasts more than two or three weeks, doesn't improve with load adjustment, limits your walking, or returns every time you start running again, get an assessment. A physical therapist can examine the knee, review your strength, and propose a progressive plan. A doctor is the right person if there are warning signs or a suspected structural injury.

The knee isn't fixed by one single thing. It's fixed with appropriate load, strength, rest, and patience. And with the humility to stop when it's time.
