The running shoe world has more acronyms than a training plan: drop, stack, rocker, plate, foam, stability, neutral. Most of those words describe real things, but not all of them matter for your case. Before buying the trendy shoe, it's worth separating what changes the experience from what only changes the brochure.

> **In short:** choose for comfort and use, not for a theory. Drop is neither good nor bad; cushioning doesn't replace strength; generic insoles don't correct an injury.

## What drop is

Drop is the height difference between the heel and the forefoot. A 10-12 mm drop is classic; 6-8 mm is intermediate; 0-4 mm is low. A low drop shifts load toward the Achilles tendon and calf; a high drop distributes more load to the knee. Neither is better in itself.

If you're coming from shoes with a high drop and want to try a low drop, do it gradually: one short run a week for several weeks. Don't debut a 4 mm drop on the day of a 20-kilometer long run. The body needs adaptation, not a religious conversion.

## Cushioning is not magic

Foam absorbs part of the impact, but it doesn't eliminate injury risk. One runner can get injured in a maximalist shoe, and another can run barefoot on grass without a problem. Cushioning is a comfort aid, not armor.

More cushioning usually means more weight and less ground feel. For long runs and hard surfaces, it can be comfortable. For intervals and short races, a lighter shoe is usually enough. If you have persistent pain, the answer isn't buying the softest shoe on the market: it's reviewing load and strength.

## Pronation: a movement, not a disease

Almost everyone pronates to some degree. Excessive pronation can be a factor in some discomfort, but it isn't an automatic diagnosis. Stability shoes can help someone with a specific, properly assessed need; for everyone else, a comfortable neutral shoe is usually the best choice.

Don't buy stability because a website told you that you "overpronate." If you're unsure, a gait analysis can provide guidance, but it isn't mandatory to start running.

## Insoles: when they make sense

Generic drugstore insoles can relieve pressure in the arch or improve fit. Custom insoles make sense in specific cases: persistent plantar fasciitis, leg-length differences, diabetes, osteoarthritis, or a podiatrist's recommendation. They are not a substitute for foot strength or a good load progression.

If you wear insoles and the pain doesn't improve in two or three weeks, go back for advice. The insole shouldn't be the only intervention.

## How to choose without losing your mind

1. **Define the use:** easy runs, intervals, trail, asphalt, walking.
2. **Prioritize comfort:** try it on with a running sock and walk, jog, and jump.
3. **Check the fit:** a centimeter of space at the big toe, a secure heel, no pressure on the instep.
4. **Don't debut them in a race:** do at least two short runs first.
5. **Budget:** an 80-100 euro shoe can be as good as a 180 euro one for a recreational runner.

## When to change

Not because of fashion or a round number. Change when the midsole is compressed, the outsole is worn, or new discomfort appears. The guide to [shoes with 900 km](/en/diario/zapatillas-900-kilometros/) has the specific signs.

And if the pain is already established, don't solve it by buying. Check your [knee](/en/diario/dolor-de-rodilla-cuando-parar/) or the [sole of your foot](/en/diario/fascitis-plantar-senales/) first and consult a professional if it doesn't improve.
